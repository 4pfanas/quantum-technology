What's next

Useful today, fault tolerant tomorrow

Current machines are noisy and sensitive to their surroundings. Classical computing grew by building in modules and running in parallel. Quantum computing is trying the same while doing useful work on the hardware it has.

Fig 7 · quantum utility, how the experiment workedIBM and UC Berkeley

The curves are a schematic of the course's description, not measured data. The idea: use a quantum computer to probe problems that challenge classical high-performance computing, then check the results classically.

IBM's Gross code (Nature paper) is roughly 10 times more efficient than earlier methods: about 288 qubits protect 12 logical qubits for around a million error-check cycles. Here each row is one logical qubit made of 12 data and 12 check qubits. The toy adds random errors each cycle. With checks on, errors are cleared every cycle. A row fails when more than 3 of its qubits go wrong at once. It does not run the real code. Experts often expect practical fault-tolerant machines to need millions of physical qubits, and error mitigation and suppression will stay useful alongside correction.

Key takeaways

What to remember

  1. Today's quantum computers are not fault tolerant.
  2. Scale, quality and speed are the three performance dimensions. Quantum Volume, CLOPS and EPLG still help for specific hardware and workloads.
  3. The IBM and UC Berkeley experiment gave evidence of reliable results at 127 qubits.
  4. Quantum-centric supercomputing treats quantum as one part of a wider high-performance computing system, with classical and quantum working as a single unit.
